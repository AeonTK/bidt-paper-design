# Guideline: Writing a Good Abstract

The abstract is the **second most frequently read part** of any paper, after the title. Hook the editor and reader here — a weak abstract loses them before they reach the introduction.

## Structure (in order)

The abstract follows the **ANSI norm** five elements, or equivalently the "winning abstract" model:

1. **Rationale / Topic** — why does this problem matter? What is the broader context?
2. **Objective / Purpose** — what is the goal of this work? What research question does it address?
3. **Methods / Materials & Methods** — what approach, technique, or study design was used?
4. **Results / Contributions** — what did you produce or discover? Be specific and concrete — quantify where possible.
5. **Implications / Conclusion** — what do the results mean? What is the takeaway for the field?

> Note: Some venues split (1) into separate Rationale and Problem elements; the functional meaning is the same.

## Rules

- **Self-contained:** no citations, no undefined acronyms, no forward references to sections.
- **No filler:** avoid "In this paper we show…" as an opener — start with the problem or context.
- **Tense:**
  - Present tense for general facts and your own contributions as standing claims.
  - Past tense for what you did (methods, experiments performed).
  - Present perfect for results you want to foreground ("We have shown…").
- **Specificity:** quantify results where possible — vague claims ("results show improvement") are weak.
- **Length:** follow the target venue's limit. Do not pad to reach a minimum.
- **No acronyms on first use without definition** — prefer spelling them out entirely in the abstract and defining them in the body. Exceptions: universally recognized acronyms (_DNA_, _RNA_) and standard chemical formulae need not be expanded.

## Norm vs. practice

| Element | ANSI norm term | "Winning abstract" term |
| ------- | -------------- | ----------------------- |
| 1       | Topic          | Rationale               |
| 2       | Purpose        | Objective               |
| 3       | Methodology    | Materials & Methods     |
| 4       | Results        | Results                 |
| 5       | Implications   | Implications            |

Both framings map to the same five functional roles. Use whichever language the venue prefers.

## Common mistakes

- Describing paper structure ("Section 2 presents…") — belongs in the introduction, not the abstract.
- Overpromising relative to what the paper actually delivers.
- Writing the abstract before the paper is done — write it last.
- Using acronyms without defining them.
- Omitting results or implications — the abstract must say what you found and why it matters.

## Checklist before finalizing

- [ ] Does it make sense without reading the paper?
- [ ] Are all five structural elements present?
- [ ] Are the contributions specific and quantified where possible?
- [ ] Does it match what the paper actually delivers?
- [ ] Are all acronyms defined or avoided?
- [ ] Is it within the venue's word/character limit?
