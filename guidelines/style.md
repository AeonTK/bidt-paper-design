# Guideline: Writing Style

## Audience orientation

Write for the reader, not for yourself. Anglo-American academic style is:
- **Audience-oriented** — structure and emphasis serve the reader's comprehension, not the author's convenience.
- **Concise** — every word earns its place.
- **Direct** — lead with the point, then support it.

The contrast with continental academic style is deliberate: continental writing buries the central message after an extended theoretical introduction; Anglo-American writing states the message first and supports it. Always lead with the claim.

**Distinguish information from message:** Raw information answers "What?" — a message answers "So what?" Do not present data without explicitly stating what it means for the reader. The interpretive step is the author's responsibility, not the reader's.

**What readers want:** clear findings, strong evidence, relevance to their work, concise summary, logical organization, credible sources, engaging presentation, and discussion of broader implications. If a passage fails any of these, revise it.

**What editors and reviewers additionally look for:** Scope (is the article appropriate for this venue?), Novelty (is this original and distinct from prior publications?), Validity (is the study well designed and executed?), Data (are results reported and analysed correctly?), Clarity (are ideas expressed clearly, concisely, and logically?), Compliance (are ethical and journal requirements met?), Advancement (is this a significant contribution to the field?).

## Sentence structure: topic and stress positions (Gopen & Swan)

Readers interpret sentences based on where information appears:

| Position | Name | What to put there |
|---|---|---|
| **Beginning** | Topic position | Short, familiar, or contextualizing information |
| **End** | Stress position | New, important, or emphasized information |

Rules:
- Put the information you want to emphasize at the **end** of the sentence.
- Put context or backward-linking information at the **beginning**.
- Avoid separating the subject from its verb — readers lose the thread.
- Information placed where readers expect it is interpreted uniformly; misplaced information creates ambiguity.

**Weak:** "The method performs well, which we demonstrate with three benchmarks."
**Strong:** "We demonstrate with three benchmarks that the method performs well."

**SVOA sentence grammar:** English sentences follow a fixed grammatical order that maps onto topic and stress positions:

| Slot | Role | Position |
|---|---|---|
| **S** Subject | Doer (active) or target (passive) | Topic — familiar information |
| **V** Verb | Action | — |
| **O** Object (direct/indirect) | Target / receiver | Stress — new information |
| **A** Adverbial (manner/place/time) | Context | Stress — new information |

Active voice places the doer in topic position; passive voice moves the target to topic position. Switching between them controls what the reader perceives as contextualizing vs. new.

A **secondary stress** falls at the **0th position** (the very opening of the sentence, before the subject). The primary stress remains at the end.

**The topic/stress framework applies at every level:**

| Level | Topic position | Stress position |
|---|---|---|
| Sentence | Subject — short, familiar information | Object/Adverbials — long, new information |
| Paragraph | Topic sentence — OLD/basic information | Concluding sentence — NEW message (main point + implication + transition) |
| Paper | Introduction — OLD/basic information | Conclusion — NEW message |

## Emphasis

Five tools for controlling what readers perceive as most important:

1. **Main clause placement** — information in the independent clause receives more emphasis than information in a subordinate clause. Place the more important idea in the main clause.
   - Less emphasis on the problem: "Although the method is slow, **it is accurate**."
   - More emphasis on the problem: "Although it is accurate, **the method is slow**."
2. **End placement** — the sentence-final stress position is the strongest; place your key point there.
3. **Length as intensifier** — a longer, more elaborate construction signals greater importance than a short one covering the same ground.
4. **Repetition** — repeating a word or phrase across sentences marks it as central to the argument.
5. **Semantic choice** — precise, specific words carry more weight than vague ones; word choice is itself an emphasis tool.

## Verb tenses

| Tense | Use for |
|---|---|
| **Simple past** | Your own methods, experiments, and procedures |
| | Detailed results — own and others' |
| | Others' work when outdated, superseded, or you disagree; when a specific past time is named |
| **Present perfect** | Your own results foregrounded as contributions |
| | Areas of inquiry with focus on recency/currency — own and others' work |
| **Simple present** | General truths, definitions, widely accepted facts |
| | Others' work when still valid or when you agree with their position |
| | Your own role as author ("We argue…", "In this paper we compare…") |
| | Abstract subjects — figures, tables, sections — avoids both first-person and passive: "Figure 2 shows…", "The results indicate…", "The next section proves…" |
| **Avoid** | Past perfect (almost never needed); progressive forms (rare in academic prose) |

**Tense choice for others' work:** use present or present perfect when you agree with their position or results; use past simple when you consider their view questionable, outdated, or when a specific date is mentioned.

Examples:
- "We **collected** 500 samples and **trained** the model for 100 epochs." (past — methods)
- "We **have shown** that X outperforms Y." (present perfect — contribution claim)
- "Figure 3 **shows** the confusion matrix." (present — figure reference)
- "The algorithm **runs** in O(n log n) time." (present — general truth)

## Active vs. passive voice

- Both are acceptable — mix them.
- **Prefer active** when the actor matters and to reduce sentence length.
- **Use passive** when the target of the action is more important than the doer, or when the doer is unknown/irrelevant.
- Passive is not inherently more "scientific" — excessive passive adds length and obscures agency.

**Active:** "We evaluated the model on three datasets."
**Passive (justified):** "The participants were randomly assigned to groups." (who assigned them is irrelevant)

## Paragraph structure

A good paragraph has three structural properties:

- **Focus** — one topic per paragraph. If a paragraph covers two topics, split it.
- **Unity** — every sentence relates to the topic sentence. If a sentence does not, move or delete it.
- **Direction** — topic sentence → supporting sentences → closing sentence with transition.

The topic sentence is the first sentence and states the paragraph's claim. The closing sentence restates the point and links to the next paragraph.

## Sentence shortening

When a sentence is too long, apply in order:

1. **Break into two** — split at the natural logical boundary.
2. **Delete filler words** — remove "it is important to note that", "as a matter of fact", "in order to" → "to", etc.
3. **Remove redundancies** — "advance planning", "past history", "each and every", "whether or not".
4. **Prefer active voice** — passive constructions add words without adding meaning.
5. **Limit nesting depth** — do not embed more than three levels of subordinate clauses inside a main clause; a fourth level is almost always too deep and should become a separate sentence. Gopen & Swan: "A sentence is too long when it has more viable candidates for stress positions than there are stress positions available."

## Golden rules

- **Write to express, not to impress** — clarity signals competence; obscurity signals confusion.
- **Pity the reader** — they are busy, reading fast, and not as familiar with your work as you are.
- **Signpost your path** — use transitions, topic sentences, and preview sentences so readers always know where they are.
- **Get to the main verb quickly** — delay between subject and verb loses readers.
- **Replace phrases with words** — "due to the fact that" → "because"; "in the event that" → "if".
- **Cut cascading clauses** — a sentence with three embedded clauses should become two or three sentences.
- **If in doubt, leave it out** — when unsure whether a detail adds value, it usually does not.

## Dangling participles

A participial phrase at the start of a sentence must modify the grammatical subject of the main clause.

**Dangling (wrong):** "Using the proposed method, the results improved significantly."
→ "the results" did not use the method.

**Correct:** "Using the proposed method, we achieved a significant improvement in results."
→ "we" used the method.

Check every sentence that begins with a verb ending in *-ing* or *-ed*.

## Hedging and certainty

Choose uncertainty expressions deliberately — readers interpret them probabilistically.

- Strong claim: "X causes Y."
- Hedged claim: "X appears to cause Y" / "X likely causes Y" / "X may cause Y."
- Avoid stacking hedges: "It could perhaps possibly suggest…" undermines credibility.
- Match your hedging level to your evidence — over-hedging is as misleading as over-claiming.

## Punctuation

### Commas

- Use a comma before a **coordinating conjunction** (FANBOYS: *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*) that joins two **complete sentences**.
- Use a comma after **introductory or transitional words/phrases/clauses** (rule of thumb: 5+ words → comma required; fewer → comma optional but often helpful).
  - "However, the results suggest…" (transitional word)
  - "In the second experiment, we observed…" (introductory phrase)
- Use commas in **horizontal lists**: "a, b, and c" (Oxford/serial comma recommended in academic writing).
- Use commas **around insertions** (non-restrictive clauses): "The model, which was trained on GPU, converged in 3 hours."

### Dashes

| Dash | Character | Use |
|---|---|---|
| **Hyphen** | `-` | Compound modifiers before a noun: *hand-held device*, *state-of-the-art method* |
| **En-dash** | `–` | Ranges (*pp. 10–20*) and compound proper nouns (*hand–eye coordination*) |
| **Em-dash** | `—` | Insertions and strong breaks: *The result—unexpectedly—exceeded baseline.* |

For insertions, prefer em-dash over commas (when you want emphasis) or parentheses (when you want to de-emphasize). Em-dash > commas > parentheses in terms of prominence.

## Citations

- **Parenthetical citation** (APA, numbered IEEE): the citation is in parentheses and does not form part of the sentence grammar. Example: "This has been demonstrated repeatedly [4]."
- **Narrative citation** (author-oriented): the author name is the grammatical subject or object. Example: "Smith et al. [4] demonstrated that…"
- Use narrative citation when the author's identity is relevant to the argument (credibility, historical context). Otherwise prefer parenthetical.
- **Reference list order** (what each entry must contain): Who → When → What → Where.
  - Who: author(s)
  - When: year
  - What: title
  - Where: venue (journal name, conference, publisher)

## Vocabulary notes

**"Data is" or "data are"?** *Data* originates as the Latin plural of *datum*. Usage splits by context:

- **Mass noun (everyday/non-scientific):** treat as singular — "This data is confusing." Replaceable with *information* or *evidence*.
- **Count noun (scientific):** treat as plural — "These data show…" / "Many of these data are…" Replaceable with *statistics* or *facts*.

In scientific writing, prefer the plural treatment: *the data are*, *these data show*. Check your venue's style guide — practices vary (IEEE Editorial Style Manual treats *data* as plural).
